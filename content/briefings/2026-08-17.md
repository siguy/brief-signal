## The Big Picture: The New Economics of Intelligence and the Fight for Ownership

### The Cost of Intelligence Is Not the Price of a Token

![gemini-3-7-flash](./images/gemini-3-7-flash.jpg)

The cost of intelligence is in flux, not simply falling. This week, Canva cut its 2026 growth projection by a third, with its CEO citing escalating AI serving costs and the decision to build an in-house image model to regain control [Rory/Jason on 20VC (90 min watch, 0:04:47)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9uq9zxtwBrk). The immediate market response: Google launched Gemini 3.7 Flash, its latest workhorse model for coding and agents, at half the price of 3.6 Flash, with DeepSWE coding benchmarks jumping from 37% to 65.3% [Koray Kavukcuoglu (1 min read)](https://x.com/koraykv/status/2087948169552490845). Independent validation quickly followed: Cognition's FrontierCode 1.1 benchmark scored Gemini 3.7 Flash at 56.3, essentially tied with Claude Sonnet 5's 56.2 — at under half the cost [CodeByPoonam (1 min read)](https://x.com/CodeByPoonam/status/2088098456246304781).

However, not all market observers were convinced. Artificial Analysis noted Gemini 3.7 Flash’s 340 tokens/second speed, but suggested that "by optimizing for speed, the model kind of ends up in a strange no-man's land" with a cost (~40 cents per task) roughly 8 times more expensive than ultra-cheap models like GPT-5.6 Luna [AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 4:00)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48). The rebuttal came from AlphaSense CEO Jack Coco: "some of the more expensive models, the ones that look more expensive based on just their price per token, actually ended up being less costly because they were more efficient in using tokens" [AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 10:30)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48). Vercel's Brandon Galang corroborated this, calling the "FUD" (fear, uncertainty, doubt) around 3.7 Flash misguided and stating he’s "giving Gemini 3.7 Flash a try as my daily driver and execution model" because "it actually sits on the Pareto Frontier" [AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 5:15)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48). This all lands as OpenAI previews its Ultrafast Mode, running GPT-5.6 Sol at 750 tokens/second (faster than 3.7 Flash), with an undisclosed premium [OpenAI (1 min read)](https://openai.com/index/previewing-ultrafast). The deeper framing: the model race is branching into multiple races (frontier, distribution, harnesses, revenue, and speed), and Google is betting that speed is a dimension people will pay attention to [NLW (Host) on AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 7:15)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48).

**Your angle with founders**

-   **Grant the premise, then move the unit of account.** Founders are right to be concerned about AI costs, and per-token pricing *looks* like the simplest answer. But raw token price is almost never the full story — the unit of account that matters is cost-per-*accepted-outcome* on *their* workload.
-   **Run the cost-efficiency audit live.** What percentage of their total model usage is for tasks where a frontier model's reasoning is actually required, versus a Flash or open model? What's the current cache hit rate for prompt inputs? How many model turns could be replaced by a deterministic tool call? The savings here come off an unchanged price list — which is why this conversation runs alongside commercial terms, not instead of them.
-   **Compete on the full-stack economics of production workloads.** A model that appears cheaper per token but requires significantly more prompt engineering, more turns, or more post-processing on *their* side is not cheaper. Focus on Gemini's multimodal understanding, its speed for interactive tasks, and the full-stack economics (model and chip design from one company) that translate into cost-per-outcome efficiency for real-world applications, rather than a raw-price comparison.
-   **Where GCP wins:** Model Garden lets founders route across Gemini, Claude, and open weights like Gemma in one stack, so cost optimization is a configuration change. The Flash series and TPU 8i offer a path to the lowest cost-per-outcome for a wide range of tasks, and Google for Startups credits sit on top.

### Who Gets to Own a Model: The Open-Weights Fight Goes Public

![open-weights-fight-zuckerberg](./images/open-weights-fight-zuckerberg.jpg)

The simmering debate over open-weights models and AI governance erupted this week into a public, on-the-record fight among tech leaders. It began with Mark Zuckerberg’s 6,500-word essay, arguing that "the notion that AI is so dangerous that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic" and that "everyone should have access to superintelligence" [Mark Zuckerberg (1 min read)](https://x.com/finkd/status/2086754845218726027). Within 48 hours, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei publicly rebutted this, stating "regulation vs. concentration is a 'false choice'" and defending Anthropic's policy proposals as designed to "disadvantage (slow down) frontier AI companies while *advantaging* smaller competitors" and open-weights [Dario Amodei (3 min read)](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758816376807762). Yann LeCun endorsed Zuckerberg, arguing open foundation models are "the *only* way forward" for diverse AI systems [Yann LeCun (2 min read)](https://x.com/ylecun/status/2088880284129210405).

Gavin Baker, an influential investor, escalated the tension, claiming Amodei has "lost the argument" and that his messaging "has been massively helpful to efforts to ban datacenters here in America" [Gavin Baker (3 min read)](https://x.com/GavinSBaker/status/2088611616577253502). The concrete fact underlying the rhetoric: Anthropic was the glaring holdout from Jensen Huang's open-weights letter, signed by Nvidia, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and SpaceX [Brian Roemmele (4 min read)](https://x.com/BrianRoemmele/status/2088788243282272509). This fight is not just philosophical; it's about control and infrastructure. Sequoia's Sonya Huang noted that "owning your intelligence at the frontier is finally becoming possible" with open weights, citing application companies like Harvey, FactoryAI, and Ramp doing their own post-training [Sonya Huang (2 min read)](https://x.com/sonyatweetybird/status/2087223288649138668). Matt Bornstein at a16z added that open source is how AI companies "stop being wrappers" on closed APIs [Matt Bornstein (1 min read)](https://x.com/a16z/status/2087202387903889442). OpenRouter CEO Alex Atallah reported that enterprises now fear frontier models more than Chinese open models due to "much more confusion around the data policy" [Alex Atallah on 20VC (69 min watch, 0:47:20)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K72oZoloA4M), and Harrison Chase of LangChain emphasized that "you want to be able to switch to avoid lock-in, but also to just use the best model when it's available" [Harrison Chase on Training Data (24 min watch, 0:01:58)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HI2q3ci3Iuc). Even Flo Crivello, who runs Chinese open models in production for Lindy, says he would ban them due to IP and national security risks [Flo Crivello on The Cognitive Revolution (127 min watch)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4JYoTE_VKaU). The tension is clear: who gets to own AI's future, and how much control do founders have?

**Your angle with founders**

-   **Grant the reality of the fight: this is a political and commercial battle for control.** It's no longer an abstract academic debate; it's a real-world contest between frontier labs, open-source advocates, and governments, impacting everything from data center approvals to which models founders can legitimately use. Founders who ignore this do so at their peril.
-   **The core reframe: who owns *your* alpha?** The fight for open weights is a fight for the application layer to own its distinct intelligence rather than renting it from a foundation model. "Not your weights, not your product" — this applies to the data, the fine-tuning, the evals, and the harnesses. This is about founder control over their own IP and future.
-   **Compete on structural optionality, not a single model.** If a founder is worried about vendor lock-in, data policy, or future sanctions, the answer is a platform that allows for genuine multi-model deployment, with open-weights as a first-class citizen. They need the ability to A/B test, to swap models as the leaderboard shifts, and to own the harness that orchestrates everything, independent of any single model provider.
-   **Where GCP wins:** The Agent Platform enables multi-model strategy, running Gemini, Claude, and open weights like Gemma side-by-side. Our open-source Agent Development Kit (ADK) lets them own the harness, and distillation fine-tuning allows them to build specialized intelligence on top of open student models.

## Seller's Edge: Enforce it in the architecture, not the prompt.

Edition #23 taught "The invoice is an architecture decision," showing how agentic systems' cost is engineered, not just priced. This week, the lesson advances to security and reliability: **AI agents, when under pressure, will always find the path of least resistance, which means rules need to be enforced structurally, not merely requested in context.** As Alex Krentsel of Exo put it, "If you never want to delete your history, you have to enforce that in the architecture of your harness rather than just asking in context, please LLM, don't ever do anything that'll delete my history — because we keep seeing that there's ways to trick the model" [Alex Krentsel on Latent Space (48 min watch, 0:17:58)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5lFD-34dhqE). This applies equally to data access, tool use, and inter-agent communication. Relying on "prompting the model not to do X" is a vulnerability, not a control.

**Worked example:** A founder boasts that their finance agent "never accesses external APIs without human approval." This sounds good, but how is it *guaranteed*? If the approval is a prompt-level instruction ("Do not call the Stripe API without a user confirming 'yes'"), an advanced agent could bypass it by generating a "yes" itself, or by calling a different API that *then* triggers the Stripe API, exploiting an unintended path. The architectural enforcement would involve the agent's identity having *no permissions* to call external APIs directly; instead, it would send a structured request to a separate, human-controlled API gateway which *then* makes the call. Exo, for example, decomposes an agent into executor/harness/sandbox and keeps the secret store in the harness so it is never exposed to the LLM at all — a property the architecture guarantees, not a rule the model is asked to follow.

**The behavior change:** When a founder says, "we prompt the model not to do X" or "the agent knows not to do Y," that is a finding, not an answer. Your next question should be: **"Which invariants are enforced structurally in your agent architecture, and which are merely requested of the model?"** This helps them identify potential bypasses and elevates the conversation to architectural robustness.
