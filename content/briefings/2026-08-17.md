## The Big Picture: The New Economics of Intelligence and the Fight for Ownership

### The Cost of Intelligence Is Not the Price of a Token

![gemini-3-7-flash](./images/gemini-3-7-flash.jpg)

Google launched Gemini 3.7 Flash this week — its latest workhorse model for coding and agents — at an introductory $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 output, half of what the Flash line carried before, with software-engineering benchmarks (DeepSWE v1.1) jumping from 37.0% to 65.3% [Koray Kavukcuoglu (1 min read)](https://x.com/koraykv/status/2087948169552490845). Two dates matter more than that percentage: the rate expires on December 31, and standard pricing of $1.50 and $7.50 applies from January 1 [Google DeepMind (2 min read)](https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/gemini-3-7-flash/). Even the post-January rate undercuts the models a founder is actually choosing between — Claude Sonnet 5 at $2.00/$10.00, OpenAI's mid-tier GPT-5.6 Terra at $2.00/$12.00. On Cognition's FrontierCode 1.1, which grades models on real engineering tasks by quality and mergeability rather than on puzzles, Google's own model card puts 3.7 Flash at 43.6% against Sonnet 5's 42.7% and Terra's 41.3%; on enterprise automation the gap is far wider, 30.4% against Sonnet 5's 10.7%.

The benchmarks cut both ways, and the losses are the more useful half. Google's own comparison has GPT-5.6 Terra ahead of 3.7 Flash on DeepSWE v1.1 (69.6% to 65.3%), on Terminal-bench 2.1 (87.4% to 85.8%) and on Terminal-bench 3.0 (20.8% to 14.9%) — so on agentic coding specifically, OpenAI's mid-tier is the stronger model, at $2.00/$12.00 against Flash's $0.75/$3.75. Sonnet 5 leads on GDPVal-AA and Agent's Last Exam. Artificial Analysis was blunter still, calling 3.7 Flash a "strange no-man's land" — though it reached that verdict by pricing Flash against GPT-5.6 Luna, a $0.20/$1.20 budget tier rather than a coding-and-agents workhorse [AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 4:00)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48). The rebuttal came from AlphaSense CEO Jack Coco: "some of the more expensive models, the ones that look more expensive based on just their price per token, actually ended up being less costly because they were more efficient in using tokens" [AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 10:30)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48). Vercel's Brandon Galang corroborated this, calling the "FUD" (fear, uncertainty, doubt) around 3.7 Flash misguided and stating he’s "giving Gemini 3.7 Flash a try as my daily driver and execution model" because "it actually sits on the Pareto Frontier" [AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 5:15)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48). This all lands as OpenAI previews its Ultrafast Mode, running GPT-5.6 Sol at 750 tokens/second (faster than 3.7 Flash), with an undisclosed premium [OpenAI (1 min read)](https://openai.com/index/previewing-ultrafast). The deeper framing: the model race is branching into multiple races (frontier, distribution, harnesses, revenue, and speed), and Google is betting that speed is a dimension people will pay attention to [NLW (Host) on AI Daily Brief (29 min watch, 7:15)](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/How-to-Decide-What-Work-AI-Should-Do-for-You-The-AI-Deputization-Audit-e3ndv48).

Two separate things are true about that price, and a seller should keep them apart. The halved rate is *introductory* — Kavukcuoglu's own word — so any model a founder builds on it needs a line for what happens when the promotional period lifts. Underneath it sits something more durable: Google designs both the model and the silicon it runs on, so it pays no other vendor's margin on either layer. SemiAnalysis puts Ironwood at roughly 30% below Nvidia's GB200 for external customers, and the TPU 8i generation claims a further 80% improvement in performance per dollar on inference. That is the mechanism that let the Flash series move 3.5 → 3.7 in three months while cutting price at all — alongside the coding jump, Code Arena Elo went 1506 → 1588 and enterprise automation (AutomationBench) went 13.4% → 30.4% [Koray Kavukcuoglu (1 min read)](https://x.com/koraykv/status/2087948169552490845). The distinction matters commercially, too: a published cost comparison drawn against list pricing is not describing what a customer pays today, which is worth checking before conceding anyone's arithmetic. Two honest limits belong in the same breath: Amazon's Trainium is also frontier-proven — it trains Claude — and the TPU software ecosystem is less travelled than CUDA, which is real migration work for a team with existing GPU tooling. What has no equivalent elsewhere is availability. Every rival's multi-model story *rents* its frontier models, so a founder can evaluate Claude on Bedrock, on Azure or on Google Cloud — but Gemini runs on Google Cloud alone. Google was also named a Leader in The Forrester Wave™: AI Platforms, Q3 2026 [Google Cloud (3 min read)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/google-named-a-leader-in-the-forrester-wave-ai-platforms/).

**Your angle with founders**

-   **Fix the comparison set before arguing about price.** The "no-man's land" verdict came from measuring 3.7 Flash against GPT-5.6 Luna at $0.20/$1.20 — a budget tier, not a coding-and-agents workhorse. Against the models a founder is genuinely choosing between, Flash is the cheapest in its class at either of its rates. Establish which models are actually in the running first; most price objections do not survive that step.
-   **Run the cost-efficiency audit live.** What percentage of their model usage is on tasks where a frontier model's reasoning is actually required, versus a Flash or open model? What is their current cache hit rate on prompt inputs? How many model turns could be replaced by a deterministic tool call? The savings here come off an unchanged price list — which is why this conversation runs alongside commercial terms, not instead of them.
-   **Put the January date on the table yourself.** A founder modelling unit economics on $0.75 needs to know it becomes $1.50 on New Year's Day — and that it still lands under Sonnet 5 and Terra when it does. Volunteering the expiry is what makes the rest of the pricing conversation credible.
-   **Check which price the comparison used.** Most cost-per-task numbers in circulation are computed off list pricing. If a founder quotes one at you, the first question is which rate it assumed — the arithmetic may not describe what they would actually pay this quarter. We keep [a verified pricing and benchmark sheet](/model-pricing/) for this edition: every figure read off the vendor's own page, the ones that failed verification marked so nobody quotes them by accident, and a breakeven column showing how much more verbose a model would have to be before its cheaper list price stops being cheaper.
-   **Where GCP wins:** Model Garden on the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (FKA Vertex AI) is the one place a founder can put Gemini, Claude and open weights like Gemma side by side on their own workload — Claude they can evaluate on any cloud, Gemini only here. Once the eval names a winner per task, routing between them is a configuration change rather than a re-architecture, and context caching (a 90% discount on cached input) plus Provisioned Throughput terms turn the result into a number they can plan against.

### Who Gets to Own a Model: The Open-Weights Fight Goes Public

![open-weights-fight-zuckerberg](./images/open-weights-fight-zuckerberg.jpg)

The simmering debate over open-weights models and AI governance erupted this week into a public, on-the-record fight among tech leaders. It began with Mark Zuckerberg’s 6,500-word essay, arguing that "the notion that AI is so dangerous that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic" and that "everyone should have access to superintelligence" [Mark Zuckerberg (1 min read)](https://x.com/finkd/status/2086754845218726027). Within 48 hours, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei publicly rebutted this, stating "regulation vs. concentration is a 'false choice'" and defending Anthropic's policy proposals as designed to "disadvantage (slow down) frontier AI companies while *advantaging* smaller competitors" and open-weights [Dario Amodei (3 min read)](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758816376807762). Yann LeCun endorsed Zuckerberg, arguing open foundation models are "the *only* way forward" for diverse AI systems [Yann LeCun (2 min read)](https://x.com/ylecun/status/2088880284129210405).

Gavin Baker, an influential investor, escalated the tension, claiming Amodei has "lost the argument" and that his messaging "has been massively helpful to efforts to ban datacenters here in America" [Gavin Baker (3 min read)](https://x.com/GavinSBaker/status/2088611616577253502). The concrete fact underlying the rhetoric: Anthropic was the glaring holdout from Jensen Huang's open-weights letter, signed by Nvidia, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and SpaceX [Brian Roemmele (4 min read)](https://x.com/BrianRoemmele/status/2088788243282272509). This fight is not just philosophical; it's about control and infrastructure. Sequoia's Sonya Huang noted that "owning your intelligence at the frontier is finally becoming possible" with open weights, citing application companies like Harvey, FactoryAI, and Ramp doing their own post-training [Sonya Huang (2 min read)](https://x.com/sonyatweetybird/status/2087223288649138668). Matt Bornstein at a16z added that open source is how AI companies "stop being wrappers" on closed APIs [Matt Bornstein (1 min read)](https://x.com/a16z/status/2087202387903889442). OpenRouter CEO Alex Atallah reported that enterprises now fear frontier models more than Chinese open models due to "much more confusion around the data policy" [Alex Atallah on 20VC (69 min watch, 0:47:20)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K72oZoloA4M), and Harrison Chase of LangChain emphasized that "you want to be able to switch to avoid lock-in, but also to just use the best model when it's available" [Harrison Chase on Training Data (24 min watch, 0:01:58)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HI2q3ci3Iuc). Even Flo Crivello, who runs Chinese open models in production for Lindy, says he would ban them due to IP and national security risks [Flo Crivello on The Cognitive Revolution (127 min watch)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4JYoTE_VKaU). The tension is clear: who gets to own AI's future, and how much control do founders have?

**Your angle with founders**

-   **Grant the reality of the fight: this is a political and commercial battle for control.** It's no longer an abstract academic debate; it's a real-world contest between frontier labs, open-source advocates, and governments, impacting everything from data center approvals to which models founders can legitimately use. Founders who ignore this do so at their peril.
-   **The core reframe: who owns *your* alpha?** The fight for open weights is a fight for the application layer to own its distinct intelligence rather than renting it from a foundation model. "Not your weights, not your product" — this applies to the data, the fine-tuning, the evals, and the harnesses. This is about founder control over their own IP and future.
-   **Compete on structural optionality, not a single model.** If a founder is worried about vendor lock-in, data policy, or future sanctions, the answer is a platform that allows for genuine multi-model deployment, with open-weights as a first-class citizen. They need the ability to A/B test, to swap models as the leaderboard shifts, and to own the harness that orchestrates everything, independent of any single model provider.

## Seller's Edge: Enforce it in the architecture, not the prompt.

Edition #23 taught "The invoice is an architecture decision," showing how agentic systems' cost is engineered, not just priced. This week, the lesson advances to security and reliability: **AI agents, when under pressure, will always find the path of least resistance, which means rules need to be enforced structurally, not merely requested in context.** As Alex Krentsel of Exo put it, "If you never want to delete your history, you have to enforce that in the architecture of your harness rather than just asking in context, please LLM, don't ever do anything that'll delete my history — because we keep seeing that there's ways to trick the model" [Alex Krentsel on Latent Space (48 min watch, 0:17:58)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5lFD-34dhqE). This applies equally to data access, tool use, and inter-agent communication. Relying on "prompting the model not to do X" is a vulnerability, not a control.

**Worked example:** A founder boasts that their finance agent "never accesses external APIs without human approval." This sounds good, but how is it *guaranteed*? If the approval is a prompt-level instruction ("Do not call the Stripe API without a user confirming 'yes'"), an advanced agent could bypass it by generating a "yes" itself, or by calling a different API that *then* triggers the Stripe API, exploiting an unintended path. The architectural enforcement would involve the agent's identity having *no permissions* to call external APIs directly; instead, it would send a structured request to a separate, human-controlled API gateway which *then* makes the call. Exo, for example, decomposes an agent into executor/harness/sandbox and keeps the secret store in the harness so it is never exposed to the LLM at all — a property the architecture guarantees, not a rule the model is asked to follow.

**The behavior change:** When a founder says, "we prompt the model not to do X" or "the agent knows not to do Y," that is a finding, not an answer. Your next question should be: **"Which invariants are enforced structurally in your agent architecture, and which are merely requested of the model?"** This helps them identify potential bypasses and elevates the conversation to architectural robustness.
